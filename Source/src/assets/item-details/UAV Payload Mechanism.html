UAV Payload Mechanism

2027 Student Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (SUAS) Competition

Figure: Isometricview of the payload mechanism.

Introduction

For the 2027 Student Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (SUAS) Competition, one of the UAV's primary objectives is the precise delivery of payloads to designated targets from an altitude of 150 feet. This task is further complicated by the requirement to deploy two distinct payloads, each differing in size and weight.

The UAV must autonomously navigate to specified target locations and selectively lower either the first or second payload based on the requirements of the drop point. Because the competition rules prohibit landing to reload, both payloads must be preloaded prior to takeoff. As a result, the hoist mechanism must be able to securely store two independent payloads and enable the controlled release of a specific payload while keeping the other safely retained until the aircraft reaches its corresponding drop zone.

Based on these requirements, I was tasked with researching, designing, and manufacturing a hoist mechanism that fulfills the needed requirements while still maintaining a light and portable form factor.

Gearbox and Drum

I chose to control the descent velocity of the payloads with cables to retain more precise control over their spatial position during delivery. A brushless DC motor winds and unwinds the cable to lift and lower the payloads.

The motor's data sheet stated that its most efficient speed is 11,000 RPM, which was far greater than required for this application. To bring that down I designed and built an 81:1 gearbox using helical gears to reduce noise and minimize wear at higher rotational speeds. This puts the carrier speed at roughly 135 RPM.

Each payload winds on its own dedicated drum. Sizing those drums has to account for the differential sitting downstream of the gearbox. The active drum turns at twice the carrier speed whenever the opposite drum is held, so at a 1 inch drum diameter the payload moves at about 70 ft/min. That puts a full 150 foot run at just over two minutes.

Open Differential

To drive two payloads independently off a single motor, I split the gearbox output with an open differential. The differential takes the single rotation coming out of the gearbox and divides it into two output paths, one per drum.

Figure: The ring gear drives the carrier, which holds the spider gears that mesh with the two side gears on the output shafts, all supported by bearing blocks on a test stand.

An open differential splits torque evenly between both outputs while letting their speeds differ. Rotation follows the path of least resistance, so blocking one output forces all of the motion into the other. Picking a payload just means picking which output is free to turn. Nothing in the drivetrain has to physically engage or disengage.

The tradeoff is speed. Carrier speed is always the average of the two outputs, so with one drum stopped the other runs at double, or roughly 270 RPM. I handled this by halving the drum diameter instead of adding another reduction stage. That keeps the payload at 70 ft/min without adding weight or another gear mesh.

Ratchet Selector

Choosing a payload comes down to choosing which differential output can turn in the direction the motor is currently driving. My approach uses a reversible pawl and ratchet on each drum.

Every drum carries a ratchet wheel and a pawl that flips between two orientations. One orientation allows clockwise rotation and blocks counterclockwise. Flip it and you get the opposite. Both pawls are positioned electronically, so each drum's allowed direction is set in software.

Selection falls out of motor direction paired with pawl orientation. Say the motor is driving clockwise. If one drum's pawl only allows counterclockwise, that drum is locked. Set the other drum's pawl to allow clockwise and it matches the motor, so that drum is free to spin. The differential sends everything to the path of least resistance, so the free drum takes all the motion and the locked drum stays put. Reversing the motor and flipping the active pawl raises that payload back up.

EE Components

By using an Arduino UNO R4 WIFI, I was able to control the motor's speed and engagment direction of the ratchet pawls. To reverse the direction of the motor, a motor driver was needed to provide this function, as well as an external 12V power supply to power all the EE components including the microcontroller. To control the position of the pawls I will be using actuator motors.

To reduce the need for special machinery or tooling and to accelerate the iteration process, most of the parts were 3D printed and incorporated off-the-shelf components such as heat-set inserts, bearings, springs, and other hardware.

Current Status

The drivetrain up to the differential outputs is built. Motor, motor mounts, the 81:1 helical gearbox, and the open differential are all done, and the assembly splits motor rotation into two independent paths like it should.

The cable drums, the ratchet selector, and the pawl control are still in progress. I am currently working on the ratchet design.
