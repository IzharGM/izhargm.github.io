FSAE Stands

FSAE Highlander Racing — adjustable car stands

Figure: The finished stands in service, holding the car at a max working height of 3 feet.

Problem

Due to the design of our Formula SAE car, the accumulator is installed and removed from the underside which requires the car be lifted every time we need to access it. Furthermore, having to work on the car at its suspension set height becomes very uncomfortable even for short periods of time. Given all this in mind, the goal of this project was to build sturdy, modular, and relatively light stands that could be used to hold the car at a range of heights and also be transported easily to different locations.

Figure: Mockup of Chassis with Accumulator installed.

Solution

With this in mind, I designed a set of welded steel stands that could hold the car at a max workable height of 36 inches and a minimum height of 20 inches while still leaving enough space on the underside for sliding jacks and for people to work without the stands getting in the way. To adjust the height, you lift the inner tube to its desired height and insert the trailer pin to lock the height in place. As an added bonus, to keep the stands from rattling, a nut was welded onto the outer tube and a bolt would thread through it to apply pressure by closing the clearance between the outer and inner tubes.

During the design of the car stands, I deliberately left jacks and leverage systems out of the design to reduce complexity and prioritize modularity. Given that the car is only 450 lbs and there are usually multiple people working near it, the car can be lifted by hand and placed on the stands without any additional tools.

Tipping Analysis

One of my main concerns when designing the stands was not the stands breaking or buckling under load but rather the stands tipping if someone accidentally leaned on the car or during the uneven removal of some of the heavier components. Because of this, the analysis was built around one question: how much force does it take to tip a loaded stand?

To account for the worst case scenario, the car was taken as 450 lb over an 84 inch span with its center of mass at (62, 7.75, 6.42) inches from the reference corner of the frame. For the stands, I simulated them weighing around 30 lb at a working height of 36 inches, and a base half width of 17 inches.

Figure: Free body diagram used for the tipping calculations.

The work was done in two steps. First, I solved for the vertical reaction at each stand, since the car's center of mass sits off center and the two ends do not carry equal load. Then I isolated a single stand with its own reaction applied and summed moments about the furthest foot from the center of mass to find the horizontal force that puts the stand on the edge of tipping. Running it that way rather than treating the whole setup as one body matters, because the stand carrying the least load is the one that tips first, and that is the number the design has to be sized against.

Figure: Reaction forces at each stand, then the tipping load solved about the foot.

Stand Placement

Stand spacing follows as a result of the first step and is crucial to maximize stability. Spacing the stands wide pushes more of the car's weight onto the far stand and leaves the near one lightly loaded and easy to tip. Pulling them in toward the center of mass evens the reactions out and raises the tipping load on the worst stand, but pull them in too far and the car starts to overhang.

The placement I settled on puts the stands 21 inches to either side of the car's center of mass, set an inch in from the end of the frame. That balances the reactions without giving up the overhang margin.

Figure: Stand placement, 21 inches either side of the center of mass.

Initial Considerations

With the method and equations in place, I ran it backwards, picking a target tipping load and asking what the stand would have to look like to avoid tipping. Every target can be met three different ways: ballast the stand, widen its base, or cap how high the car is allowed to go.

  • 100 lbf: add 100 lb of ballast, or a 34 inch base, or cap the working height at 30 inches.
  • 150 lbf: add 285 lb of ballast, or a 52 inch base, or cap the working height at 20 inches.
  • 200 lbf: add 460 lb of ballast, or a 68 inch base, or cap the working height at 15 inches.

Ballast was out, since the whole point is lightweight stands. Capping the height defeats the purpose of the project. That left base width, and 100 lbf at a 34 inch base was the combination that stayed portable, so the base grew from 24 inches to 34 inches which the hand calculations proved would work best.

Figure: The three ways to hit each tipping target, and the clamped down case at the right.

Clamping the car down to the stands was checked as a separate case. Tying the car and stand together lets the car's weight resist the tipping moment instead of riding on top of it, and at the original 24 inch base that came out to 70.6 lbf at a 42.62 inch push height.

Modifications

The widened base was the main change, which meant the stands had to be reworked. Afterwards, I added a pair of side stoppers to each stand to keep the car from sliding off. I also added a rubber sheet to the top of each stand to protect the frame from scratches. Finally, I added square plates and rubber feet to the bottom of each stand to increase the friction between the stand and the floor.

Build

Before starting the build, I first designed and built a fixture using a 3/4 inch sheet of plywood and 3D printed guides to hold the steel members, ensuring they were at the correct angle and the correct length relative to the entire stand, and that all four stands were built identically. The row of holes up each inner tube is the height adjustment, and the pins that carry the car drop straight through those. Once everything checked out, they were painted red, which is how they sit in the shop today.

Figure: One stand laid out in the fixture before welding, with the 3D printed guides setting the angle and length of every member so all four came out identical.

Figure: The stands after welding was finished, showing the pinned height adjustment and the widened base.

Validation

Prior to manufacturing, I ran a Static Study in SolidWorks before committing to the geometry. Probing the reaction at a stand foot in the assembly returned 128 lbf, which lined up with the hand calculations and gave me confidence that the free body diagram matched what the model was actually doing.

Figure: Reaction force probed at the foot of a loaded stand.

The height adjustment pins were the other thing worth checking, since they carry the whole car through a half inch of steel. I ran a static study on the pin joint with 600 lbf spread across four pins, 150 lbf each, fixing the bottom of the 1.75 by 1.75 by 0.083 outer tube. In 4130 steel, which is good for about 97.2 ksi ultimate normal and 62 ksi ultimate shear, the pin saw 562 psi normal and 802 psi shear. That works out to a factor of safety of 173 in normal stress and 77 in shear.

Those numbers are well within the material and geometry limitations of the pins and stands, which confirms that these stands can withstand the weight of the car and more.

Figure: Static study of the adjustment pin, showing a peak normal stress of 562 psi.

Cost

The early concept was priced against the sawhorses you can buy at a hardware store, and at roughly 40 feet of tubing it came in around $150. It was more expensive than an off-the-shelf sawhorse, but store-bought stands do not adjust and do not give the height range the accumulator install needs.

The final build came in lower. The legs take 34 feet of 1.5 by 1.5 by 0.083 tube plus 4 feet of 1.75 by 1.75 by 0.083 for the sliding section at $84.46, the side stoppers add $10.02, and the pins are $10 for a pair, with rubber sheet for the grips on top of that. Total build cost is about $110.

The stands were also drawn with later use in mind. Nothing about the design is specific to this car, and there is room to add a leveling system if a future project needs one.

Things I would change

Overall, the stands worked as expected and haven't had any issues whatsoever. That being said, if I were to build another set, I would run a static study analyzing them from the front and not just the side like the hand calculations covered. My intuition was that since the base is wider in that direction, tipping wasn't really something I needed to worry about there. The side profile is what has the smaller footprint, so that's the case I put more emphasis on. Furthermore, I would also want to run a buckling analysis on the stands. I originally didn't do it since the load on the stands plus the material specs and geometry didn't really cause concern that they could buckle under load. However, it still would have been good to have in order to give a definitive max load the stands can withstand to ensure they are not overloaded.
