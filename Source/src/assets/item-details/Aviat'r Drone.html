Background

This is an ongoing project aimed to be mission ready for the 2027 Student Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (SUAS) Competition. A primary mission of the UAV is the accurate delivery of payloads to designated targets from an altitude of 150 feet.

To ensure proper and efficient execution of this task, the drone must carry several essential components, including a flight controller that processes sensor data to make fine adjustments for stable flight, a 3-axis gimbal that scans the surrounding terrain and provides visual feedback for navigation, a payload mechanism capable of holding and deploying two objects to their designated locations, an onboard computer which serves as the mind of the drone to avoid collisions with other drones and navigate autonomously across the vast terrain, as well as other critical electrical systems.

A rigid, lightweight drone that can efficiently package and protect these components from environmental exposure, withstand impact in the event of a crash, and handle the aerodynamic and structural loads experienced in flight is vital to the successful completion of the competition objectives.

Chassis

The drone chassis is arguably the single most critical component of the entire system, and it reflected that importance in the time, research, and testing that went into designing it. The goal was to create a structure that could compactly house all the necessary components, withstand the forces experienced during flight, both with and without payload, and allow for rapid part replacement in the event of a crash or when adapting to a new situation.

Figure: The bare chassis, showing the three stage centerpiece, dovetailed arms, and landing gear.

My main method of manufacturing relied heavily on 3D printing, which allowed the initial version of the chassis to have a very modular design. This greatly sped up the iteration process and made it much faster to replace broken pieces and adapt the drone to the situation at hand. For the centerpiece, I settled on a 3 stage system that allowed for easy assembly and disassembly of the drone through 4 columns at each corner, held in place with heat set inserts and allen bolts. Once the centerpiece of the chassis was settled, the next step was figuring out the best way to attach the drone arms to it, which meant finding the most structurally sound way to ensure the arm's weakest point, its connection to the center, could withstand the forces it would experience both during flight and in the case of a crash.

Figure: Exploded view of the chassis, highlighting the modular three stage stack and dovetail arm connections.

I ultimately went with a dovetail style connection between the arms and the center body, since it relied on the material's own strength to keep the arm from moving longitudinally away from the center. Since most of the force and stress occurred in the vertical direction, relying on the material's own strength was enough to handle the longitudinal loads, while allen bolts provided the clamping force needed to keep the arm secured to the center vertically and provide the rigidity needed.

Finally, when it came to the design of the drone arm itself, I used a cantilever beam as my foundation and validated it for drone use by performing a static analysis, which allowed me to simulate the arm's behavior and the maximum stress it could expect to see, based on the force applied at one end, the geometry of the arm, and the material's specifications. From there, I used topology optimization to reduce the weight of the arm by 70% while still retaining more than enough strength and rigidity for it to function properly.

Flight Controller, GPS Module, and PDM

For the flight controller, we went with the Holybro Pixhawk 4 mainly because of its reliability and the failsafe behaviors it supports through the PX4 firmware. Once set up correctly, its ability to autonomously hover in place and safely land in the event of signal loss gave me peace of mind that the drone wouldn't just fall out of the sky if something went wrong. The kit we bought also came with HolyBro's own M10 GPS module, which gave the drone spatial awareness for navigation and failsafes, and a power distribution module that handled power delivery and battery monitoring, feeding the Pixhawk the data it needed to trigger safety failsafes like low battery RTL.

Onboard Computer

For the onboard computer, we went with the NVIDIA Jetson Nano mainly because of its ability to run AI models directly on the drone rather than relying on an off board system. Its GPU allowed for real time image processing and computer vision tasks like object detection, which would've been way too demanding for the Pixhawk to handle on its own. It also had solid community support and documentation, which made getting the vision pipeline up and running a lot smoother.

Motors and ESCs

When choosing motors, given the nature of the competition which requires the drone to fly upwards of 8 miles and the amount of weight it would need to carry, I knew that efficiency would play a major role in choosing the right motors, only second to them being able to provide enough thrust. Given these requirements, I went with the T-Motor F90 Long Range with a KV rating of 1300. The F90 is built specifically for long range and cinematography applications, which made it a great fit since it deals in the same realm.

Having a lower KV means it could spin bigger, more efficient 7-in props at a lower RPM, which means more thrust per watt compared to a higher KV motor of the same size. It also had enough torque due to its larger stator to handle the payload weight without needing to sit near max throttle just to hover, which from my findings is usually where efficiency starts to tank.

Power

Given the F90 KV1300's 5-6S voltage range, I paired it with a 6S 1000mAh 100C Ovonic LiPo with an XT60 connector. Since the motor is rated for 5-6S, running it at 6S lets me pull the full voltage range out of it and get the most performance out of the motor without overdriving it.

The 100C discharge rate was also important since T-Motor's own bench data shows the F90 KV1300 can pull up close to 53.6A under load, so having a battery capable of dumping 100A continuously meant the battery wouldn't sag under load or bottleneck the power the motors were trying to draw, keeping voltage stable throughout the flight. On top of that, the battery's compact and lightweight design, at around 174g, helped keep the overall weight down, which mattered since every gram not spent on the battery pack was a gram I could put toward payload or extra flight time.

Current Status and Next Steps

At this stage, the drone is mostly operational in manual mode, controlled through a FlySky FS-16 transmitter paired with its respective receiver. While the core platform is flying, there are still a few issues that need to be addressed before moving into more advanced testing. During manual flights, the drone will occasionally lose signal, and since the failsafe behaviors like stabilized automatic hover haven't been configured yet, this is something I plan to prioritize soon to ensure the drone can respond safely if signal is lost again.

My current focus, however, is on developing the payload mechanism responsible for lifting and lowering the payload during flight. Since this is a highly custom mechanism with no off the shelf solution to fall back on, it's taking a good amount of time and iteration to get right. Once that system is finalized and tested, the next phase will involve integrating the gimbal along with the autonomous hardware needed to give the drone its full autonomous capabilities.

Alongside this, I'm also looking to bring down the weight of the drone frame, since it currently sits at around 400g, as well as clean up the cable management throughout the build to improve reliability and make future maintenance and troubleshooting easier.
