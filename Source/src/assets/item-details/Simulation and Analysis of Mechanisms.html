Simulation and Analysis of Linkage Mechanisms

Solving for position, velocity, and acceleration of three linkages in MATLAB

Figure: Solved link angles across a full revolution of the input crank on the six-bar linkage.

Objective

The goal for this project was to build 3 MATLAB programs, one for each mechanism, that take their respective linkage and output the position, velocity, and acceleration of each link at every step of a complete revolution of the input crank. Each mechanism has its own program, written as its own file.

The three mechanisms get progressively more complex: a four-bar linkage, an offset slider-crank, and a six-bar multi-loop linkage. Each one has one degree of freedom by Gruebler's equation, so once the input link is prescribed the rest of the motion is fully determined. What the three programs do share is the method, since every one of them comes down to writing a vector loop, solving it with Newton-Raphson, and then differentiating the same equations for velocity and acceleration.

The Mechanisms

The four-bar is the base case: four links and four revolute joints, converting rotational motion into oscillating or back and forth motion. It consists of a fixed frame, crank, coupler, and rocker, which in the diagram are R1, R2, R3, and R4, with R2 as the input crank. The ground, crank, coupler, and rocker are assigned length values of 100, 30, 90, and 80 respectively, with the input crank starting at 20 degrees, and the coupler and rocker angles are what get solved for. I also ran an offset version, where the fixed pivot O4 is placed at a vertical offset from O2, so the ground link is no longer perfectly horizontal. Four-bar linkages are used in machines such as windshield wipers, pumps, presses, and robotic arms.

Figure: The four-bar linkage, with the ground offset marked at the O4 pivot.

The slider-crank converts rotational motion into reciprocating linear motion instead. It consists of a crank, connecting rod, slider, and fixed frame, which in the diagram are the crank OB, the connecting rod BC, and the block at C as the slider. The crank is assigned a length value of 2 and the connecting rod a value of 4, and the slider moves horizontally along a path offset by a value of 0.5 above the crank's center of rotation at O. That offset changes the slider's position, speed, and timing during the cycle compared to a standard slider-crank, which is run with an offset of zero for comparison. The crank angle, angular velocity, and angular acceleration are treated as the knowns, and the connecting rod angle and the slider position are solved as the outputs. Offset slider-cranks are commonly used in engines, pumps, compressors, and machines that need controlled straight-line motion.

Figure: The offset slider-crank, with the slider path offset above the crank pivot.

The six-bar is where it gets harder. It has six links and seven revolute joints, counting the fixed pivots and the joints at A, B, and C, across two closed loops sharing a common link. The fixed ground link includes the frame connecting the pivots at O2, O4, and O6, and L2 through L6 are the moving links. This type of mechanism is useful because it can create more complex and coordinated motion than a basic four-bar linkage.

The moving links are the input crank L2 at a length of 2, the coupler L3 at 5, the shared link L4 at 4, and then L5 at 5 and L6 at 4.5 carrying the motion through the second loop. The ground is fixed, so it is written as two vectors between the three fixed pivots: L1 at 5 from O2 to O4, held at 0 degrees, and L7 at 4.5 from O4 to O6, held at 10 degrees. Those two are the reason seven lengths show up in the equations for what is still a six-link mechanism. The angles of links 3, 4, 5, and 6 are the unknowns.

Figure: The six-bar linkage, with the two loops sharing the link at O4.

Input Motion

The input crank is prescribed as a function of time, and the input angle is swept from 0 to 360 degrees so the crank completes one full revolution. The constant speed case is steady rotation, with the crank angle equal to the elapsed time, the angular velocity fixed at 1 rad/s, and the angular acceleration zero.

The non-constant case adds a sinusoidal term on top of that steady rotation, giving a crank angle of t + 0.25 sin(2t), an angular velocity of 1 + 0.5 cos(2t), and an angular acceleration of -sin(2t). The crank speeds up and slows down within each revolution and carries a nonzero angular acceleration throughout. The four-bar and the slider-crank are both analyzed under constant and non-constant angular velocity, while for the six-bar the input angular velocity is assumed constant and the input angular acceleration is set to zero.

Newton-Raphson and Jacobian methods

Every mechanism here reduces to the same idea. Walking around a closed loop of links and returning to the start means the vectors sum to zero, which gives two scalar equations from the x and y components. Those equations are nonlinear in the unknown angles, so there is no closed form to rearrange into.

To solve this, I used the Newton-Raphson method, which is basically a numerical way to find the roots of a nonlinear system, meaning the input values that make a set of equations equal zero, by starting with a guess and refining it step by step. I used it here because the loop closure equations for the linkage are nonlinear, they have sines and cosines of the joint angles in them, so there's no clean algebraic way to just solve for the exact configuration. Newton-Raphson gets around that by linearizing the system at each guess and moving closer to the actual solution each time, similar to how you'd use a derivative to find the slope at a point and use that to step closer to where a single equation crosses zero.

The difference here is that I don't have just one equation and one unknown, I have a whole system of loop closure equations and a whole set of unknown joint angles, so a single slope isn't enough. That's where the Jacobian comes in. It's basically the multi-variable version of a derivative, a matrix where each entry tells you how much one equation changes when you nudge one specific unknown. So instead of one slope, I get a full grid of slopes that captures how every equation responds to every unknown at once. Newton-Raphson uses that matrix to figure out which direction and how far to move all the joint angles together so that all the residual equations shrink toward zero at the same time.

With that in mind, the loop closure equations are written as a residual that should be zero at the correct configuration, the Jacobian of that residual with respect to the unknowns is formed, and the correction is solved from the linear system and applied. That repeats until the correction falls below tolerance, which usually only takes a few iterations per timestep. The four-bar derivation below is the whole method on one page, and the other two mechanisms follow the same steps with their own equations.

Figure: The four-bar worked all the way through, from the vector loop to the acceleration equations. The unknown vector is the coupler and rocker angles, and the standard case sets the ground angle to zero.

Velocity and acceleration come out of the same equations rather than needing a separate method. Differentiating the loop closure once with respect to time gives a system in the unknown angular velocities, and differentiating again gives one in the angular accelerations. In the matrix form written above, the matrix on the left of the velocity system is the same Jacobian that was formed to solve position.

Program 1: The Four-Bar

The four-bar program solves the mechanism in three parts. Each part references the user-entered link lengths and input motion and uses Newton-Raphson to solve the unknown angles. Part A simulates a standard four-bar with no offset, and since the input crank has non-constant speed, angular acceleration is included and accounted for. It solves for the coupler and rocker angles over time and creates plots and an animation to show the movement at different points in time.

Part B solves an offset four-bar by setting an offset value on one ground pivot, then uses Newton-Raphson again to find the coupler and rocker angles for the new offset geometry. Part C simulates that offset mechanism at two different input motions, constant speed and non-constant speed, and compares the two using plots and animations.

In Part A the input crank angle increased over time in a curved shape because angular acceleration was accounted for, and the angular velocity increased linearly, which showed that the crank is moving at a non-constant speed. The solved coupler and rocker angles changed in a uniform manner over time, which proved that they move continuously as the input crank rotates. Since the angle curves did not have any sudden large jumps, the motion can be taken as stable and physically realistic, and in the simulation the four links remain connected throughout the motion. In Part C the two input cases showed clearly different behavior: at constant speed the crank angle changes at a steady rate, while at non-constant speed the crank spun faster as time increased, which caused the output link angles to change more quickly later in the simulation.

Figure: Coupler and rocker angles over time under non-constant input speed.

Program 2: The Offset Slider-Crank

The slider-crank program starts by having the user input their desired values, those being the link lengths, the offset, the input angle, the angular velocity, and the angular acceleration. Using those values it solves for the unknowns, the connecting rod angle and the slider position, working from the crank-slider position equations. Since those equations are nonlinear, it uses Newton-Raphson to find the correct values.

Figure: Loop closure and the Newton-Raphson setup for the offset slider-crank. The unknown vector is the connecting rod angle and the slider position, and the offset is the right hand side of the y equation.

Once the positions are found, the code takes the derivative of the position equations and uses the velocity equations to find the slider and connecting rod velocities. It then takes the derivative of the velocity equations and uses the acceleration equations to find the slider and connecting rod accelerations. Finally it plots the results and animates the mechanism to show how the crank-slider moves.

Figure: The slider-crank Jacobian, and the velocity and acceleration equations that come from differentiating the position equations in each direction.

As the crank rotated about the fixed pivot, it drove the connecting rod, which then pushed and pulled the slider along a straight horizontal path. The slider position plot shows it moving smoothly back and forth as the crank angle changed, reaching its maximum position when the crank was extended toward the slider and its minimum when the crank rotated back toward the opposite side. Because the mechanism has an offset, the motion was slightly uneven compared to a standard crank-slider with no offset: the offset shifts the slider path vertically, which causes the forward and return motion to not be perfectly symmetric. There were no sudden jumps or breaks in the plots, so the mechanism stays connected during the full cycle.

Figure: Slider position with and without the offset, showing the asymmetry it introduces.

Program 3: The Six-Bar Multi-Loop

The six-bar program is also initiated by having the user enter their desired values for the mechanism, such as the link lengths, the fixed ground angles, the input angle, the input angular velocity, and the input angular acceleration. Link 2 is used as the main input link, and the code solves the unknown motion of the other links from it.

Before anything can be solved, the two loops have to be written separately. The first runs from O2 out to A, on to B, back through O4 and home, and the second runs from O4 through B to C, on to O6 and back. Every link is written as a vector with a length and an angle, and each loop is set to sum to zero, which gives an x and a y equation per loop.

Figure: The two loops written out, and the position equations that come from setting each one to zero.

Stacking those four equations gives one residual with four unknowns, the angles of links 3, 4, 5, and 6, which is what Newton-Raphson is handed to find the position angles. The angle of link 4 appears in both loops, which is the shared link that ties the two together.

Figure: The four unknown angles and the four residual equations solved together at every timestep.

The code then uses those position results to find the angular velocities and angular accelerations by taking the derivative of the position equations, loop by loop. As the crank turns, links 3 and 4 move first through the first loop, and then that motion carries links 5 and 6 through the second loop. After the calculations are complete, the code creates plots for position, velocity, and acceleration, and animates the mechanism to show how the links move.

Figure: Velocity and acceleration for both loops, with the first loop feeding the second.

The position plot shows that the link angles change continuously with the input angle, which means the mechanism is not jumping between positions and that Newton-Raphson is solving the angles correctly. The velocity and acceleration plots also follow smooth patterns, which shows how each link speeds up, slows down, and changes direction as the input link rotates. The animation confirms what the graphs show: the links stay connected at the joints, and the mechanism returns to its starting position after one full rotation of the input crank.

Figure: The six-bar at 0 and at 360 degrees of input, back in the same configuration.

Animation

All three programs animate their mechanism on top of the plots. The animation is driven off the solved angles rather than being a separate calculation: each frame rebuilds the joint coordinates from the link lengths and the angles at that timestep, then redraws the links and the joints.

Figure: The animation loop for the four-bar, building the ground points and joints A and B from the link lengths and the solved angles before plotting the links. The three program files are open across the top.

What I Would Change

A possible future improvement would be to have the code allow the user to add or subtract the number of links, and then run the program to see how that affects the movement of the mechanism. That would turn these three programs into one.
