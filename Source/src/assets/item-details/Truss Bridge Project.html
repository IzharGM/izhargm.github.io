Truss Bridge Project

Maximizing load carried per gram of structure

Figure: The finished bridge, with the printed fixture it was assembled on sitting in front of it.

Introduction

The objective was a truss bridge built entirely from polypropylene drinking straws, supported at both ends, loaded at midspan, and scored on how much load it carries relative to how much it weighs. Nothing about the structure is judged in isolation, only that ratio, so every gram of material has to be earning its place and any member that is not carrying load is actively costing points.

The figure of merit was structural efficiency, the mass equivalent of the load the bridge carries divided by the mass of the bridge itself. Working from a typical bridge weight of 24 grams, we set a target of 245.5 and a load range of roughly 12 to 20 pounds.

The approach was to work the problem in three passes that each check the one before it. Hand calculations to find where the load actually goes and what the members can take, simulation to compare arrangements that hand calculation cannot separate, and a purpose built fixture so that the structure that gets glued together is the one that was analyzed.

Member Forces

The first pass was the method of joints, run by hand on the side view. The design is symmetric, so solving half of it describes the whole structure. At a 13 pound load the distribution comes out very lopsided: the members directly above the load carry the bulk of it, up to 31.75 pounds in the worst member, while one vertical sits at zero.

Figure: Member forces from the method of joints at a 13 pound load, on half the symmetric side view.

That result set the priorities for everything after it. A zero force member is a candidate for removal or for being repurposed as bracing, and the heavily loaded top members are where the design either survives or does not.

Buckling Calculation

Straws in compression do not fail by crushing, they fail by buckling, so the governing check was slenderness. Comparing the slenderness ratio of the members against the transition value for the material put the design in the Euler regime rather than the Johnson regime, which set which critical load formula applies.

Figure: Slenderness check and the Euler critical load, giving 5892.5 grams, about 13 pounds.

Running the Euler critical load gave 5892.5 grams, roughly 13 pounds. We treated that as a bound rather than a prediction. Hand calculation on a structure with this many joints leaves room for arithmetic error, so the number was there to sanity check the scale of the answer while SolidWorks did the comparison work.

Layout Study

This was where the real gains were. The side truss geometry was settled early, but the members connecting the two side trusses were not, and where those sit changes the entire failure picture. I built variants that moved those members to different locations along the span and changed their orientation, then ran every variant through a buckling study at the same 12 lbf load so the results could be compared directly against each other.

Figure: An early arrangement, buckling at a load factor of 0.219.

Looking at the same variant from above makes the problem obvious. The two side trusses are splaying away from each other at midspan and pinching back in, which is the connecting members failing to tie the two sides together where the load goes in. The bridge is not failing as one structure, it is failing as two panels that happen to be near each other.

Figure: Top view of the same mode, showing the two sides spreading apart at midspan.

Moving the connecting members and reorienting them pushed the load factor up to 0.373, and the mode shape changed character. Instead of the whole midspan opening up, the deformation concentrated in the top chord directly above the load, which is where the method of joints said the highest forces were. Getting the failure back into the member that is genuinely the most loaded meant the bracing was finally doing its job.

Figure: A better arrangement at a load factor of 0.373, with the failure moved into the top chord.

The best combination reached a load factor of 0.622, close to three times the worst arrangement for no change in material. All of the variants weighed about the same, so the entire gain came from where the connecting members went and how they were oriented, not from adding straws.

Figure: The layout we built, at a load factor of 0.622.

I was using the model comparatively rather than as an absolute prediction. The load factors are conservative against what the bridge actually did on the test rig, but the ranking between layouts is what the decision needed, and the ranking is the part a model like this gets right.

Failure Prediction

With the layout settled, a static study at 20 lbf was used to pin down where the bridge would let go and how much it would move getting there. Peak resultant displacement came out at 14.3 mm, and the highest combined axial and bending stress landed on the top chord directly above the load.

Figure: Static displacement at 20 lbf, peaking at 14.3 mm under the load point.

Figure: Axial and bending stress, concentrated in the top four members above the load.

The static study, the buckling study, and the hand calculation all pointed at the same failure site, the top four members directly above the load. Three independent methods converging on the same answer was the point at which the design was worth committing material to.

Build

Building a truss out of straws by eye does not work. The joints have to land in the right place or the geometry that all of the analysis assumed is not the geometry that gets built, so I designed a 3D printed fixture in the shape of the side truss with the member paths recessed into it. The straws drop into those pockets, get held down while the joints set, and every side panel comes out identical.

Figure: A side panel being laid up in the printed fixture, clipped down while the joints set.

The fixture went through its own revisions alongside the bridge. The version shown here holds the members down at every node, which was the fix for panels coming out of the earlier fixture with the chord lifting away from the profile.

Result

The bridge that came out of it weighed 21 grams and held 19 pounds. By the same efficiency measure we set the target with, that is a ratio of roughly 410, against a goal of 245.5.

Almost none of that came from adding material. The variants were within a gram of each other, so the result was bought with member placement and orientation, which cost nothing.
