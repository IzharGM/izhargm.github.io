FSAE Stands

FSAE Highlander Racing — adjustable car stands

Figure: The final stand, with the widened 34 inch base and pinned height adjustment.

Problem

The accumulator on our Formula SAE car installs from underneath, which means the car has to be lifted before that job can even start. With the car sitting on the ground there is almost no clearance to work with, so every install turned into a session of lying under the frame and working overhead. It was uncomfortable, slow, and the kind of setup where a slip puts someone underneath a 450 pound car.

Figure: The frame with the accumulator mounted underneath, the component that drove the whole project.

Solution

Working alongside a teammate, I designed a set of four welded steel stands that hold the car at a workable height while leaving the entire underside open. Height is set by a pinned inner tube, so the same stand covers everything from a low working height up to full accumulator clearance.

We deliberately left jacks and leverage systems out of the design. The car gets lifted by hand, which keeps the part count and the cost down, and it means there is no hydraulic system to maintain or fail while someone is underneath. The tradeoff is that lifting has to be done as a team and done properly, since the load is entirely on the people doing the lift.

Figure: The frame supported on all four stands.

Figure: Side view showing the clearance left underneath for installing and removing the accumulator.

Tipping Analysis

The failure mode that actually matters here is not the stand breaking, it is the stand tipping. Someone leaning on the car or pushing a stuck component sideways applies a horizontal load high above a narrow base, and that is what puts the car on the floor. So the analysis was built around one question: how much sideways force does it take to tip a loaded stand?

The car was taken as 450 lb over an 84 inch span with its center of mass at (62, 7.75, 6.42) inches from the reference corner of the frame, each stand at 30 lb, a working height of 36 inches, and a base half width of 17 inches.

Figure: Free body diagram used for the tipping calculations.

The work was done in two steps. First I solved for the vertical reaction at each stand, since the car's center of mass sits off center and the two ends do not carry equal load. Then I isolated a single stand with its own reaction applied and summed moments about the downwind foot to find the horizontal force P that puts the stand on the edge of tipping. Running it that way rather than treating the whole setup as one body matters, because the stand carrying the smallest reaction is the one that tips first, and that is the number the design has to be sized against.

Figure: Reaction forces at each stand, then the tipping load solved about the foot.

Stand Placement

Stand spacing falls straight out of that first step. Spacing the stands wide pushes more of the car's weight onto the far stand and leaves the near one lightly loaded and easy to tip. Pulling them in toward the center of mass evens the reactions out and raises the tipping load on the worst stand, but pull them in too far and the car starts to overhang.

The placement I settled on puts the stands 21 inches to either side of the car's center of mass, set an inch in from the end of the frame. That balances the reactions without giving up the overhang margin.

Figure: Stand placement, 21 inches either side of the center of mass.

Sizing the Base

With the method in place I ran it backwards, picking a target tipping load and asking what the stand would have to look like to survive it. Every target can be met three different ways: ballast the stand, widen its base, or cap how high the car is allowed to go.

  • 100 lbf: add 100 lb of ballast, or a 34 inch base, or cap the working height at 30 inches.
  • 150 lbf: add 285 lb of ballast, or a 52 inch base, or cap the working height at 20 inches.
  • 200 lbf: add 460 lb of ballast, or a 68 inch base, or cap the working height at 15 inches.

Ballast was out, since the whole point is a stand one person can carry. Capping the height defeats the purpose of the project. That left base width, and 100 lbf at a 34 inch base was the combination that stayed portable, so the base grew from 24 inches to 34 inches.

Figure: The three ways to hit each tipping target, and the clamped down case at the right.

Clamping the car down to the stands was checked as a separate case. Tying the car and stand together lets the car's weight resist the tipping moment instead of riding on top of it, and at the original 24 inch base that came out to 70.6 lbf at a 42.62 inch push height.

Modifications

The widened base was the main change, and the feet were the other one. The first design bolted the feet to the floor, which fixed the tipping problem by removing the stand's ability to move at all, but it also meant the stands could only live in one place. I swapped that for rubber sheets bonded to the feet, which gives enough grip to keep the stand from sliding while leaving the set portable.

Validation

I checked the statics against SolidWorks before committing to the geometry. Probing the reaction at a stand foot in the assembly returned 127 lbf, which lined up with the hand calculations and gave me confidence that the free body diagram matched what the model was actually doing.

Figure: Reaction force probed at the foot of a loaded stand.

The height adjustment pins were the other thing worth checking, since they carry the whole car through a half inch of steel. I ran a static study on the pin joint with 600 lbf spread across four pins, 150 lbf each, fixing the bottom of the 1.75 by 1.75 by 0.083 outer tube. In 4130 steel, which is good for about 97.2 ksi ultimate normal and 62 ksi ultimate shear, the pin saw 562 psi normal and 802 psi shear. That works out to a factor of safety of 173 in normal stress and 77 in shear.

Those numbers are enormous, and that is the point. The pins are nowhere near being the limiting part of this design, which confirmed that tipping, not pin strength, is what the stand should be designed around.

Figure: Static study of the adjustment pin, showing a peak normal stress of 562 psi.

Cost

The early concept was priced against the sawhorses you can buy at a hardware store, and at roughly 40 feet of tubing it came in around $150. It was more expensive than an off the shelf sawhorse, but store bought stands do not adjust and do not give the height range the accumulator install needs.

The final build came in lower. The legs take 34 feet of 1.5 by 1.5 by 0.083 tube plus 2 feet of 1.75 by 1.75 by 0.083 for the sliding section at $84.46, the side stoppers add $10.02, and the pins are $10 for a pair, with rubber sheet for the grips on top of that. Total build cost is about $110.

The stands were also drawn with later use in mind. Nothing about the design is specific to this car, and there is room to add a leveling system if a future project needs one.
