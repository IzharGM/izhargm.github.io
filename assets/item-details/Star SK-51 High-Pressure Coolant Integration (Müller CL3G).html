Star SK-51 High-Pressure Coolant Integration (Müller CL3G)

Integrating a Müller CL3G high-pressure coolant unit into a Star SK-51

Figure: The Müller unit in relation to the SK-51, with the machine's own coolant pumps and filters in the foreground.

Problem

The Star SK-51 needed higher pressure coolant running through it to properly cool the tools we were using, especially since a lot of our drills are extremely small and have tiny through holes for coolant delivery. With drills that small, standard coolant pressure isn't enough to actually get fluid through the hole and onto the cutting edge where it's needed. Higher pressure was also necessary to help clear the chips being produced, since without enough force behind the coolant, the chips can't be pushed off the tool effectively, which leads to recutting, poor chip evacuation, and a worse surface finish. Given all this, the goal was to integrate a Müller CL3G high-pressure coolant unit into the SK-51 to provide the pressure needed for both proper cooling and clean chip removal.

Hydraulic and Electrical Considerations

On the hydraulic side, the first step was going through the OEM schematic for the SK-51 to understand how the existing coolant system was laid out and to figure out what fitting sizes, hose sizes, and pressure ratings we would need for the new lines running between the Müller unit and the machine. Once I understood the existing setup, I made a new schematic that reflected the changes we wanted and showed how everything would actually be run going forward. One of the SK-51's existing coolant pumps, the one supplying high pressure to Turret 1, was removed and that line was rerouted to the Müller instead. Turret 2 and the sub spindle presented a bigger issue, since both were fed off the same high pressure line. We wanted the sub spindle to keep running off the SK-51's OEM pump while Turret 2 ran off the Müller, so those two lines had to be separated and made fully independent of each other before anything else could be connected. I presented the updated schematic to the Senior Manufacturing Quality Engineer for review, and then used it again when the hose and fitting supplier came in so I could accurately walk him through exactly what we needed.

Figure: The hand drawn schematic before the final version, working out the runs to Turret 1, Turret 2, and the sub spindle off the Müller pump and the coolant tank, with the adapter and hose size called out at each connection.

Figure: The lines tagged at the machine end, including the sub spindle and Turret 2 flushing feeds, so the shared runs could be told apart before being separated.

The electrical and programming work came out of how the SK-51 handles coolant control. The machine uses its own solenoid valves to determine whether high pressure runs through a given line, but the Müller already has its own valve block doing the same job. Rather than run coolant through both sets of valves, the goal was to let the Müller handle the switching directly, which meant the SK-51 had to be able to operate normally without its own valves being in the path first. That required electrical modifications so the machine's control signals could drive the Müller's valve block instead of its own solenoids, along with programming changes so the SK-51 would still see the coolant system as functioning correctly and wouldn't fault out or behave unexpectedly during operation.
