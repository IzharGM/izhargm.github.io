Truss Bridge Project

Maximizing load carried per gram of structure

Figure: The finished bridge with the printed fixture used to assemble it.

Objective

The goal of this project was to build a truss bridge made entirely out of drinking straws that would be supported at both ends and loaded at its center. What made this project different others is that it would be scored in terms of how much load it could hold relative to the weight of the bridge and how accuarte your prediction of the failure method/point given a certain load was to the actaul results. That meant that every member on the bridge needs to be dilligently analyzsed through both hand calculations and similuations to ensure that the memeber was actually increasing the strenght of the bridge but not at the cost of reducting its relative strenght factor. Furtheremore, accuracy of failure method and load could only be confidenlty confirmed after extensive analysis.

Approach

With these requirements in mind, the first step was obtaining the most accurate material specifications of the drinking straws and then using calipers to get the outer and inner diameter of the straw's cross-sectional area. I would then take this information and import it into Solidworks' weldment library to make a custom weldment using both the geometry and material specs found, to ensure that the static and buckling analysis gave an accurate depiction of my exact straws under load.

After confirming that, the next step was to reference a pre-existing Parker bridge when building a 3D sketch of my bridge in Solidworks and perform continous static and buckling analyses until I reached my desired relative strength factor. I would play around with different member positions and use more or less members to optimize the bridge for my specific purpose. It wasn't guesswork but rather analyzing the stress concentrations and areas where the bridge buckled, and either adding an additional member, modifying the orientation of the member, or completly changing the bridge design to reduce those high buckling areas and the stress found at certain areas under a certain load.

Method of Joints and Sections Analysis

When incorporating hand calcualtions, the method of joints and method of sections were utilized to further add to the credibilty of our values. Given that the design is symmetric, solving half of it can accuralety describes the whole structure. At a 13 pound load the distribution came out very lopsided. The members directly above the load carry the bulk of it, up to 31.75 pounds in the worst member, while one vertical sits at zero.

Figure: Member forces from the method of joints at a 13 pound load, on half the symmetric side view.

That result set the priorities for everything after it. A zero force member is a candidate for removal or for being repurposed as bracing, and the heavily loaded top members are where the design either survives or doesn't.

Buckling Calculation

Straws in compression won't fail by crushing, they will fail first by buckling, so the governing check was slenderness. Comparing the slenderness ratio of the members against the transition value for the material put the design in the Euler regime rather than the Johnson regime, which set which critical load formula applies.

Figure: Slenderness check and the Euler critical load, giving 5892.5 grams, about 13 pounds.

Running the Euler critical load gave 5892.5 grams, which is roughly 13 pounds. That being said, I treated that as a bound rather than a prediction. Hand calculations on a structure with this many joints leave plenty of room for arithmetic error, so the number was there to sanity check the scale of the answer while SolidWorks did the comparison work.

Static and Buckling Simulation Analysis

This is where the most accurate values could be expected to see. The side truss geometry was settled early, but the members connecting the two side trusses were not, and where those sit changes the entire failure picture. Because of this, I built variants that moved those members to different locations along the span and changed their orientation, then ran every variant through a buckling study at the same 12 lbf load so that the results could be compared directly against each other.

Figure: An early arrangement, buckling at a load factor of 0.219.

Looking at the same variant from above makes the problem obvious. The two side trusses are splaying away from each other at midspan and pinching back in, which is the connecting members failing to tie the two sides together where the load goes in. In other words, the bridge isn't failing as one structure, it's failing as two panels that happen to be next to each other.

Figure: Top view of the same mode, showing the two sides spreading apart at midspan.

Moving the connecting members and reorienting them pushed the load factor up to 0.373, and the mode shape changed character. Instead of the whole midspan opening up, the deformation concentrated in the top chord directly above the load, which is where the method of joints said the highest forces were. Getting the failure back into the member that is genuinely the most loaded meant the bracing was finally doing its job.

Figure: A better arrangement at a load factor of 0.373, with the failure moved into the top chord.

The best combination reached a load factor of 0.622, which is close to three times the worst arrangement for no change in material. All of the variants weighed about the same, so the entire gain came from where the connecting members went and how they were oriented, and not from adding straws.

Figure: The layout we built, at a load factor of 0.622.

It is worth mentioning that I was using the model comparatively and not as an absolute prediction. The load factors are conservative against what the bridge actually did on the test rig, but the ranking between the layouts is what the decision needed, and the ranking is the part a model like this gets right.

Failure Prediction

With the layout settled, the last question was where the bridge would actually let go. A static study at 20 lbf put peak resultant displacement at 14.3 mm and landed the highest combined axial and bending stress on the top chord directly above the load, and the buckling studies were there to check that answer from a different direction. Both plots below are the first mode shape at a 12 lbf load, and in both of them the amplitude collects in the same place.

Figure: Buckling amplitude for mode shape 1 at 12 lbf, load factor 0.554. The deformation collects in the top chord and the cross bracing directly above the load point, drawn at a deformation scale of 2.48.

Figure: The same failure site seen from above, mode shape 1 at 12 lbf and a load factor of 0.373. The top chord and the bracing across midspan carry the highest amplitude while the two end bays sit near zero, at a deformation scale of 1.44.

The static study, the buckling study, and the hand calculations all pointed at the same failure site, which was the top four members directly above the load. Having three independent methods land on the same answer was the point where the design was worth committing material to and a designated failure point too.

Assembly Fixture

Before any straws got cut, the fixture was modeled with the finished truss sitting in it so the two could be checked against each other. The profile plate carries the outline of the side truss and the clamps come down on the members from above, which is what keeps the built geometry the same as the geometry every study was run on.

Figure: The CAD model of the side truss seated on its fixture, with the profile plate in red and the clamps holding the members down against it.

Build

Building a truss out of straws by eye doesn't work. The joints have to land in the right place or the geometry that all of the analysis assumed is not the geometry that gets built. Because of this, I designed and printed a fixture in the shape of the side truss with the member paths recessed into it. The straws drop into those pockets, get held down while the joints set, and every side panel comes out identical.

Figure: A side panel being laid up in the printed fixture, clipped down while the joints set.

The fixture went through its own revisions alongside the bridge. The version shown here holds the members down at every node, which was the fix for panels coming out of the earlier fixture with the chord lifting away from the profile.

Result

The bridge that came out of it weighed 20 grams and held 19 pounds. By the same efficiency measure we set the target with, that is a ratio of roughly 430.

Almost none of that came from adding material. The variants were within a gram of each other, so the result was bought with member placement and orientation, which cost nothing.

Video: The load test, with the bridge pulled down against the frame until it failed.

Things I would change

Overall, the bridge came out well and beat the target by a wide margin. That being said, If I were to do this project again, the first thing I would change is how the joints were treated. Every model I ran assumed the members were connected perfectly, but the glued joints are the part I had the least visibility into, and on a structure this light they are where most of the uncertainty actually is. Furthermore, I would want to figure out why the buckling studies came out so conservative. The layout we built buckled at a load factor of 0.622 in simulation but the bridge held 19 pounds on the rig, so in theory it should have failed under 18lbs but didnt. Knowing where that gap comes from would let me trust a model like this for sizing and not just for comparison. Finally, I would build the fixture earlier. It went through its own revisions while the bridge design was still moving, and the panels that came off the first version had the chord lifting away from the profile, which is exactly the kind of error the fixture was supposed to prevent in the first place.
