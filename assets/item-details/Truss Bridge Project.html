Truss Bridge Project

Maximizing load carried per gram of structure

Figure: The finished bridge, with the printed fixture it was assembled on sitting in front of it.

Problem

The goal of this project was to build a truss bridge made entirely out of polypropylene drinking straws that would be supported at both ends and loaded at midspan. What made this project different from most is that nothing about the bridge is scored on its own, only how much load it carries relative to how much it weighs. Because of this, every gram of material has to earn its place and any member that is not carrying load is actively costing points.

The figure of merit was structural efficiency, which is the mass equivalent of the load the bridge carries divided by the mass of the bridge itself. Working off a typical bridge weight of 24 grams, we set a target of 245.5 and a load goal of 8 kilograms.

Approach

With this in mind, I worked the problem in three passes that each check the one before it. First, hand calculations to find where the load actually goes and what the members can take. Then simulation to compare the arrangements that hand calculations cannot separate. Finally, a purpose built fixture so that the structure that gets glued together is the same one that was analyzed.

Member Forces

The first pass was the method of joints, run by hand on the side view. Given that the design is symmetric, solving half of it describes the whole structure. At a 13 pound load the distribution came out very lopsided. The members directly above the load carry the bulk of it, up to 31.75 pounds in the worst member, while one vertical sits at zero.

Figure: Member forces from the method of joints at a 13 pound load, on half the symmetric side view.

That result set the priorities for everything after it. A zero force member is a candidate for removal or for being repurposed as bracing, and the heavily loaded top members are where the design either survives or doesn't.

Buckling Calculation

Straws in compression don't fail by crushing, they fail by buckling, so the governing check was slenderness. Comparing the slenderness ratio of the members against the transition value for the material put the design in the Euler regime rather than the Johnson regime, which set which critical load formula applies.

Figure: Slenderness check and the Euler critical load, giving 5892.5 grams, about 13 pounds.

Running the Euler critical load gave 5892.5 grams, which is roughly 13 pounds. That being said, I treated that as a bound rather than a prediction. Hand calculations on a structure with this many joints leave plenty of room for arithmetic error, so the number was there to sanity check the scale of the answer while SolidWorks did the comparison work.

Layout Study

This is where the real gains were. The side truss geometry was settled early, but the members connecting the two side trusses were not, and where those sit changes the entire failure picture. Because of this, I built variants that moved those members to different locations along the span and changed their orientation, then ran every variant through a buckling study at the same 12 lbf load so that the results could be compared directly against each other.

Figure: An early arrangement, buckling at a load factor of 0.219.

Looking at the same variant from above makes the problem obvious. The two side trusses are splaying away from each other at midspan and pinching back in, which is the connecting members failing to tie the two sides together where the load goes in. In other words, the bridge isn't failing as one structure, it's failing as two panels that happen to be next to each other.

Figure: Top view of the same mode, showing the two sides spreading apart at midspan.

Moving the connecting members and reorienting them pushed the load factor up to 0.373, and the mode shape changed character. Instead of the whole midspan opening up, the deformation concentrated in the top chord directly above the load, which is where the method of joints said the highest forces were. Getting the failure back into the member that is genuinely the most loaded meant the bracing was finally doing its job.

Figure: A better arrangement at a load factor of 0.373, with the failure moved into the top chord.

The best combination reached a load factor of 0.622, which is close to three times the worst arrangement for no change in material. All of the variants weighed about the same, so the entire gain came from where the connecting members went and how they were oriented, and not from adding straws.

Figure: The layout we built, at a load factor of 0.622.

It is worth mentioning that I was using the model comparatively and not as an absolute prediction. The load factors are conservative against what the bridge actually did on the test rig, but the ranking between the layouts is what the decision needed, and the ranking is the part a model like this gets right.

Failure Prediction

With the layout settled, I ran a static study at 20 lbf to pin down where the bridge would let go and how much it would move getting there. Peak resultant displacement came out at 14.3 mm, and the highest combined axial and bending stress landed on the top chord directly above the load.

Figure: Static displacement at 20 lbf, peaking at 14.3 mm under the load point.

Figure: Axial and bending stress, concentrated in the top four members above the load.

The static study, the buckling study, and the hand calculations all pointed at the same failure site, which was the top four members directly above the load. Having three independent methods land on the same answer was the point where the design was worth committing material to.

Build

Building a truss out of straws by eye doesn't work. The joints have to land in the right place or the geometry that all of the analysis assumed is not the geometry that gets built. Because of this, I designed and printed a fixture in the shape of the side truss with the member paths recessed into it. The straws drop into those pockets, get held down while the joints set, and every side panel comes out identical.

Figure: A side panel being laid up in the printed fixture, clipped down while the joints set.

The fixture went through its own revisions alongside the bridge. The version shown here holds the members down at every node, which was the fix for panels coming out of the earlier fixture with the chord lifting away from the profile.

Result

The bridge that came out of it weighed 20 grams and held 19 pounds. By the same efficiency measure we set the target with, that is a ratio of roughly 430 against a goal of 245.5.

Almost none of that came from adding material. The variants were within a gram of each other, so the result was bought with member placement and orientation, which cost nothing.

Video: The load test, with the bridge pulled down against the frame until it failed.

Things I would change

Overall, the bridge came out well and beat the target by a wide margin. That being said, if I were to do this project again, the first thing I would change is how the joints were treated. Every model I ran assumed the members were connected perfectly, but the glued joints are the part I had the least visibility into, and on a structure this light they are where most of the uncertainty actually is. Furthermore, I would want to figure out why the buckling studies came out so conservative. The layout we built buckled at a load factor of 0.622 in simulation but the bridge held 19 pounds on the rig, so the ranking between the layouts was right even though the absolute numbers were nowhere close. Knowing where that gap comes from would let me trust a model like this for sizing and not just for comparison. Finally, I would build the fixture earlier. It went through its own revisions while the bridge design was still moving, and the panels that came off the first version had the chord lifting away from the profile, which is exactly the kind of error the fixture was supposed to prevent in the first place.
