UAV Payload Mechanism

2026 Student Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (SUAS) Competition

Figure: Isometricview of the payload mechanism.

Introduction

For the 2026 Student Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (SUAS) Competition, one of the UAV's primary objectives is the precise delivery of payloads to designated targets from an altitude of 150 feet. This task is further complicated by the requirement to deploy two distinct payloads, each differing in size and weight.

The UAV must autonomously navigate to specified target locations and selectively lower either the first or second payload based on the requirements of the drop point. Because the competition rules prohibit landing to reload, both payloads must be preloaded prior to takeoff. As a result, the hoist mechanism must be able to securely store two independent payloads and enable the controlled release of a specific payload while keeping the other safely retained until the aircraft reaches its corresponding drop zone.

Based on these requirements, I was tasked with researching, designing, and manufacturing a hoist mechanism that fulfills the needed requirements while still maintaining a light and portable form factor.

Gearbox and Drum

Controlling the descent velocity of the payloads through the use of cables was chosen to retain more precise control over the payloads' spatial position during delivery. A brushless DC motor was used to wind and unwind the cable to lift and lower the payloads.

Given that the motor's data sheet stated that its most efficient speed is 11,000 RPM, which was far greater than required for this application, I designed and built an 81:1 gearbox which incorporated helical gears to reduce noise and minimize wear at higher rotational speeds. This brought the output speed down to roughly 135 RPM which, when paired with its custom cable drum, resulted in a linear payload velocity of approximately 70 ft/min, allowing each payload to be raised or lowered in just over two minutes.

Spring-Applied Brake

To reduce stress on the motor while holding a payload stationary, I designed and manufactured a spring-applied brake capable of providing 20 N·m of holding torque. This brake reduces the load placed on the motor when it is not rotating and also serves as a safety-critical fail safe. In the event of an electrical or control system failure, the brake mechanically engages to prevent any unintended payload motion.

The brake is disengaged by rotating an arm attached to the brake pad. As the arm rotates, it winds a spring and stores energy, pulling the brake pad away from the disc rotor and allowing free motion of the system. To actuate this arm, I initially explored using a variable reluctance stepper motor, since it could apply torque when powered while offering minimal resistance when unpowered. However, a motor that was sufficiently compact, strong, and cost-effective could not be identified.

To work around the detent torque of a standard stepper motor when unpowered, I designed a pawl and ratchet freewheel mechanism. This system meshes with a one-way sprocket, allowing torque to be transmitted in a single direction to disengage the brake and hold that position. When power is removed, the stored energy in the wound spring drives the mechanism in the opposite direction, allowing free rotation and automatically re-engaging the brake.

Clutch

To control which payload was deployed while the other remained secured, I designed and manufactured a custom clutch-style mechanism that allows the gearbox to selectively engage or disengage from each payload's individual drum. This approach enabled independent control of each payload using a single motor and gearbox assembly, which reduces system complexity and the number of parts needed.

EE Components

By using an Arduino UNO R4 WIFI, I was able to control the motor's speed, disengagement of the brake, and the clutch's ability to engage and disengage each drum. To reverse the direction of the motor, a motor driver was needed to provide this function, as well as an external 12V power supply to power all the EE components including the microcontroller.

To reduce the need for special machinery or tooling and to accelerate the iteration process, most of the parts were 3D printed and incorporated off-the-shelf components such as heat-set inserts, bearings, springs, and other hardware.
