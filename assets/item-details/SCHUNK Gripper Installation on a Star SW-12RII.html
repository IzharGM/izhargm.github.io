SCHUNK Gripper Installation on a Star SW-12RII

Setting finished parts onto the conveyor instead of dropping them

Problem

On our Star SW-12 Swiss lathes, once a part finishes machining it gets dropped onto the conveyor belt rather than gently placed on it. For a lot of parts that would be fine, but the contacts we make are highly precise and very prone to bending, so any impact from being dropped introduces real risk of deformation. On top of that, the drop and the contact with the belt can leave surface scratches on the part, which matters just as much on components where surface finish is part of the spec. A part that comes off the machine within spec can end up out of spec by the time it reaches the container, which means the issue isn't caught until inspection, well after the machine has already run a full batch the same way.

To solve this, the goal was to install SCHUNK pneumatic grippers on the machines so that the part is grabbed as soon as it's finished and then gently set down on the conveyor belt instead of falling onto it. This removes the drop entirely and gives the part a controlled handoff from the machine to the belt, which protects both the geometry and the surface finish we just spent the whole cycle producing, and cuts down on scrap caused purely by material handling rather than by the machining itself.

Installation and Routing

The installation itself involved getting into the inner sections of the machine in order to route both the pneumatic lines and the electrical cable needed for the SW-12 to communicate with the gripper. The electrical side is what tells the gripper when to open and close, so the timing lines up with the end of the cycle and the part gets grabbed at the right moment rather than at random. Routing all of this meant working around existing components inside the machine and finding paths for the lines that wouldn't interfere with anything already there or create a wear point over time.

Calibration

Calibration was its own problem. To get the machine to know exactly where to go to place the part into the gripper, I had to use a method called floating calibration, which involves synchronizing two dynamically moving parts so they end up working as one. In this case those two parts are the gripper and the spindle. Since neither one is fixed in place, you can't just teach a single static position and be done with it. Both have to be brought into agreement with each other so the handoff happens in the same spot every cycle, regardless of where either one is coming from on its own.

Scaling It Across the Floor

The bigger challenge is scale. We have over a hundred machines, and each one needs this system installed, so it wasn't enough for me to just learn how to install and calibrate the gripper myself. I also had to write a detailed instruction manual describing the entire process so that anyone else could do the install without needing me there. That manual needs to rely on clear, precise visuals rather than photographs, because photos of the inside of a machine are genuinely hard to interpret. The interior is cramped and full of similar looking components, lighting is poor, and there's no clean way to isolate the one bracket or port you're actually pointing at. If someone doesn't already know where things are, a photo gives them almost no sense of orientation, and they end up guessing which part of the image actually matters.

Reverse Modeling the SW-12

The problem is that no CAD model of the SW-12 is available anywhere, so I had to build one myself. That meant going back and forth to the machine to take measurements and reverse model it with enough detail to accurately show the user where they need to be inside the machine and what they need to do once they're there. With a model, I can cut away sections, isolate specific components, and orient views exactly how I need them, which makes each step in the manual unambiguous in a way a photograph can't be.
